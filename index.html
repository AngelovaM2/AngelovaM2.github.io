Digital twin back end

Bachelor final project (Software Engineering Project) for a research lab at TU/e · 2025 · team of 11

  • Python
  • FastAPI
  • MQTT
  • PostgreSQL
  • Docker
  • pytest
  • GitLab

A digital twin is a virtual copy of a physical place that stays up to date with real sensor data, in this case buildings on the TU/e campus shown in a 3D model. The lab gave us the back-end server of their platform, but most of its endpoints were broken, nothing was validated and there were barely any tests or documentation. Our job as a team was to refactor it into something the lab and future students could actually build on.

What I did

  • Built the MQTT message broker part of the API: connecting to the broker, subscribing to all sensors and actuators of a twin, publishing ON/OFF commands to actuators and listing the received messages and publishers.
  • Connected the broker to the PostgreSQL database with a background loop that stores incoming sensor readings, at an interval that can be changed through the API.
  • Wrote the broker's unit tests with pytest (22 tests over three test files).
  • Cleaned up mistakes in other parts of the team's code, for example user roles and database permissions, the query helper for the graph database and the building file conversions.
  • Set up the Docker Compose stack together with the team (API, PostgreSQL and GraphDB containers).
  • Wrote big parts of the requirements, design and test documents, based on our meetings with the client.

As a team we took the repository from almost no tests to 89% test coverage. The code belongs to the lab, so it is not public, but I am happy to walk through my part during an interview.

Blind-spot warning device for bicycles

USE Engineering Design · TU/e · 2023 · team of 3

  • C++ (Arduino)
  • ESP32-C3
  • UART
  • I2C
  • LiDAR
  • GPS
  • OLED
  • Soldering

A small device that warns a cyclist when something comes up from behind. A LiDAR distance sensor is mounted at the back of the bike, and the unit on the handlebar shows your speed and the time, with a big warning sign and a beep when a car or another cyclist gets too close or approaches too fast. Two people left our group in the first weeks, so the three of us who stayed did a bit of everything.

What I did

  • Wrote most of the GPS code: reading the GPS module over UART, showing the speed in km/h and the local time on the OLED screen, and showing "??" instead of a speed when the signal quality (HDOP) is too poor to trust.
  • Made the sound warnings on my own: a slow beep and a faster, more urgent one.
  • Wrote the LiDAR code together with a teammate: reading the sensor at 20 Hz, turning the change in distance into an approach speed and smoothing it with a moving average before deciding to warn.
  • Drew the warning sign as a custom font character, so the OLED can draw it in one go.
  • Assembled and soldered the circuits and tested every sensor on its own before combining them.

The hardest part was the microcontroller. We started with a Raspberry Pi Pico, ran into compatibility problems, switched to an Arduino Uno that turned out too slow to read the LiDAR and draw the screen at the same time, and ended up with an ESP32-C3.

Sorting robot and its simulation

DBL Embedded Systems · TU/e · 2022 · team of 6

  • Java
  • Webots
  • Blender
  • Raspberry Pi

A Raspberry Pi robot that sorts black and white discs coming down a conveyor belt, with one arm for each colour that pushes the disc off the belt. Green discs act as "unknown objects" and are let through. The user can choose to sort all discs or only a chosen number of each colour.

What I did

  • Built a virtual copy of the robot in Webots, so we could test the sorting logic without needing the hardware.
  • Modelled the robot's parts in Blender (frame, pistons, gears, motor body) and put them together in the simulated world.
  • Wrote the controller in Java: two simulated cameras read the colour of the disc in front of each arm, linear motors push it off, and position sensors tell the code when an arm has reached the end, so it can pull back and count the disc.
  • Implemented both modes ("sort all" and "sort some") and a green light that turns on when a green disc passes.
  • Ran experiments and research with the team. The Python code on the physical robot was written by my teammates.

MetApocalypse

A puzzle game set in the MetaForum building at TU/e, made to get incoming computer science students excited about first-year topics. The player collects machine parts by solving puzzles built on real course material, such as logic gates and state machines.

What I did

  • Designed and programmed the logic gate puzzle: the player picks up AND, OR and NOT gates and places them into slots on a circuit panel. When every slot holds the right gate, the panel opens and gives a reward.
  • Built a tutorial that teaches the puzzle in three steps (AND, then AND and OR, then all three), opening the next gate cabinet each time.
  • Programmed the character movement, animations and footstep sounds, and the pick-up system for parts, notes and puzzle items.
  • Made the main menu, and together with a teammate the checklist and journal UI.
  • Drew the puzzle's pixel art and animations in Aseprite, which made me fall in love with pixel art on another level.

Connecting scripts with Unity's attributes was a parade of oops moments, but in the end every playtester solved the puzzle. Their feedback also showed me what was missing: a clear flow direction on the circuit and a hint to start the tutorial.
